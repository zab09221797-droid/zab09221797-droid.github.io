Shanghai Travel Guide: The Bund, Food and Everything In Between

Last updated: June 2026 · 7 min read

Shanghai is the city that surprises people who think they already know what China looks like. It's cosmopolitan in a way that feels genuinely earned — a place where art deco architecture sits beside futuristic skyscrapers, where French bakeries and Shanghainese noodle shops share the same block, and where the nightlife runs later than almost anywhere else in the country.

The Bund

The Bund is Shanghai's most famous view — a waterfront promenade lined with colonial-era buildings facing the gleaming towers of Pudong across the Huangpu River. It looks best at night when everything is lit up, but the morning is quieter and better for photographs without crowds. Walk the full length from Waibaidu Bridge south to Xintiandi, about 1.5km, and take your time with the architecture.

The view from the other side — from the Pudong waterfront or from the observation decks of the Shanghai Tower or Oriental Pearl — gives a completely different perspective. The Shanghai Tower at 632 meters is the tallest building in China; the observation deck on the 118th floor costs ¥180 and the views on a clear day are extraordinary.

The French Concession

The French Concession is where Shanghai becomes genuinely pleasant to just walk around. Tree-lined streets, art deco apartment buildings, independent coffee shops, vintage clothing stores and some of the city's best restaurants are all packed into this neighborhood. Wukang Road is the most photographed street — the curved building at the junction is on every Instagram post about Shanghai — but the surrounding blocks are just as good.

Tianzifang, a warren of converted 1920s shikumen houses turned into shops and cafes, is worth an hour. Xintiandi nearby is the more upscale version — better restaurants, higher prices, still worth seeing.

Food in Shanghai

Shanghainese food is sweeter and richer than most Chinese regional cuisines — braised pork belly (hongshao rou), soup dumplings (xiaolongbao) and pan-fried pork buns (shengjian bao) are the things to eat here. Nanxiang Steamed Bun Restaurant in the Old City has been making xiaolongbao since 1900 and the queues are worth it. For something less touristy, any local breakfast shop will serve shengjian bao fresh from the pan for around ¥12.

Shanghai's restaurant scene is genuinely international in a way few Chinese cities match — you can eat exceptionally well from dozens of cuisines without trying. The French Concession has the highest density of good restaurants; budget around ¥150–300 per person for a proper dinner.

Getting Around

Shanghai's metro is one of the best in the world — fast, clean, cheap and covering almost everywhere you'll want to go. Fares start at ¥3. The Maglev train from Pudong Airport to Longyang Road station takes 8 minutes at 431 km/h and costs ¥50 — worth doing once for the experience. From there, take the metro into the city center.

Best Time to Visit

Spring (March–May) and autumn (October–November) are the best seasons. Summer is brutally hot and humid with frequent typhoons. Winter is cold and damp but manageable. The city is genuinely busy year-round — there's no quiet season in Shanghai.
