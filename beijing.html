Beijing Travel Guide: What to See, Eat and Do in 2026

Last updated: June 2026 · 8 min read

Beijing is one of those cities that takes you completely by surprise. You expect history — and you get it, in overwhelming quantities — but what catches most visitors off guard is how alive the city feels. Ancient hutong alleys sit next to gleaming subway stations. Street food vendors set up beside Michelin-starred restaurants. It's a city that refuses to be just one thing.

The Forbidden City

The Forbidden City — officially the Palace Museum — is the obvious starting point, and it earns every minute you give it. Set aside a full morning at minimum. The sheer scale catches most people off guard: 980 buildings, 8,700 rooms, and a layout so deliberately overwhelming that it was designed to make visitors feel small. Book tickets online in advance at pm.cn — the daily limit is 80,000 visitors, and popular dates sell out weeks ahead.

The palace opens at 8:30am and closes at 5pm (4:30pm in winter). Enter from the south gate on Tiananmen Square, exit from the north into Jingshan Park, where you can climb the hill for a full aerial view of the golden rooftops — one of Beijing's best photographs.

The Great Wall

Which section you visit matters more than most guides admit. Badaling is the most famous and the most crowded — expect shoulder-to-shoulder crowds on weekends and holidays. Mutianyu is the better choice for most visitors: well-restored, far less crowded, and accessible by cable car if the steep sections aren't appealing. Jinshanling and Jiankou attract hikers looking for unrestored sections with dramatic crumbling towers, but require more planning to reach.

From central Beijing, Mutianyu takes about 90 minutes by bus or hired car. The wall itself is open from 7:30am to 5pm. Go on a weekday if you can.

Temple of Heaven

The Temple of Heaven complex is where Beijing locals actually spend their mornings — doing tai chi, playing cards, singing Peking opera, and flying kites in the surrounding park. Arrive before 9am and you'll see the city at its most relaxed. The Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests, with its triple-layered circular roof, is one of the most photographed structures in China for good reason. The park is open 24 hours; the temple buildings open at 8am.

Food Worth Seeking Out

Peking duck is the obvious answer, and Quanjude and Dadong are the names everyone mentions — but Da Dong on Dongsishitiao is worth the price for the leaner, less greasy version that changed how the dish is prepared across the city. For something more everyday, jianbing (savory crepes filled with egg, crispy wonton and chili sauce) are eaten for breakfast on almost every street corner and cost around ¥10.

The Wangfujing Snack Street is a tourist trap — overpriced and performative. The hutong neighborhoods around Nanluoguxiang and Beihai Park have better food at more honest prices.

Getting Around

Beijing's subway is cheap, fast and covers almost everything you'll want to see. A single journey costs ¥3–¥7 depending on distance. Download the Beijing Subway app and load a transit card with cash at any station — the card works on buses too. Taxis are metered and generally honest; DiDi (China's Uber) works well if you have a Chinese phone number.

Practical Tips

Beijing's air quality varies enormously by season. Spring (April–May) and autumn (September–October) are the best times to visit — mild temperatures and relatively clear skies. Summer is hot and humid with occasional heavy rain. Winter is cold and dry, often hazy, but the Great Wall covered in snow is genuinely spectacular.

Most major attractions require advance online booking since COVID-era capacity limits became permanent policy. Buy tickets before you arrive in Beijing, not on the day.
