Wandering Beijing: My Notebook on What to See, Eat, and Figure Out

Last updated: June 2026 · 12 min read

Beijing is one of those massive, sweeping cities that completely dwarfs you the second you arrive. I still remember stepping out of the train station on my very first trip, looking up at the endless concrete ring roads, and feeling a sudden wave of panic. It moves at an incredible, hyper-modern speed, yet ancient courtyard neighborhoods sit quietly right around the corner from neon-lit skyscrapers. It's a place that refuses to belong to just one century. Figuring out how to move through it smoothly takes a bit of preparation since the entire city runs on a digital real-name verification network, but once you get a handle on the day-to-day rhythm, it becomes an incredible place to explore.

Surviving the phone setup and packing for the miles

A lesson I learned incredibly fast is that your original physical passport or national identity card is a non-negotiable ticket to everything here. Beijing uses a strict real-name identity setup. This means you must carry your physical ID on your person at all times because you'll be checked when walking into hotel lobbies, entering any major sight, or passing through public security lanes. If you happen to have a valid student ID card, senior citizen credentials, military paperwork, or disability documents, throw them in your backpack. Most state-backed parks offer a direct 50% discount tier or completely free entry for cardholders. One thing that surprised me was how often these entry gates ask for the actual physical copy, so snapping a clear photo of both sides of your ID and storing it on your phone is a smart safety backup, but it won't replace the real thing at the gate.

Before you even leave your hotel room, you'll need a few applications loaded onto your phone. The absolute lifecycle app here is YiTongXing. It generates a simple transit QR code on your screen that allows you to scan right past the turnstiles on every subway and bus line without buying physical single tokens. Pair that with Amap or Baidu Maps because typical Western navigation apps are completely lost here when trying to find small shops or alleyways. Make sure WeChat Pay or Alipay are linked to a working card ahead of time because the city is almost entirely cashless. There's no need to carry thick stacks of paper money; sliding 50 or 100 RMB in small physical bills into your pocket for rare emergency edge cases is plenty.

On that first trip, I made the terrible mistake of packing fashionable leather boots instead of actual running shoes. By day two, the miles of walking across rough imperial stone paths completely tore my feet apart. Leave the formal shoes behind and wear your most supportive athletic sneakers. Packing also requires checking the seasonal patterns. Spring and autumn offer gorgeous mild afternoons but bitterly chilly nights, so throwing a light jacket into your daypack is smart. Summer brings intense UV rays and sudden tropical downpours—high-SPF sunscreen, a wide hat, and a solid umbrella are lifesavers. Winter is a dry, biting cold that stings your face, requiring a heavy down coat, thermal layers, a thick scarf, and gloves. I always carry a portable power bank, basic stomach meds, and heavy-duty band-aids. Also, bring a reusable water bottle; free filtered water dispensers are tucked inside nearly every major historical site and subway terminal, which saves a fortune on plastic bottles.

A Quick Neighborhood Trick

Spend a few minutes mapping out your daily route by geographic proximity before walking out the door. Grouping nearby historic neighborhoods together keeps you from wasting half the afternoon caught in traffic loops across the giant multi-ring layout.

The realities of crowd control and avoiding scams

Watching thousands of commuters quietly stream through a multi-line subway interchange during morning rush hour without a single collision is fascinating. However, visiting the heavy-hitting historic spots requires serious forward planning. Major landmarks enforce strict daily capacity caps, and they don't keep spare tickets at the gate once allocations sell out online. If you just show up hoping to buy a ticket at the entrance, security will turn you away. I also learned a frustrating lesson on my first trip: the Forbidden City, National Museum, Chairman Mao Memorial Hall, and most public galleries are firmly closed on Mondays. Plan your week carefully so you don't end up staring at a locked brass gate.

Keep your wits about you when walking around crowded tourist hubs like Tiananmen Square or the Great Wall exits. Aggressive ticket scalpers or illegal private tour operators often approach people offering "guaranteed internal tickets," "exclusive entry lanes," or "secret ways to skip the lines." Don't listen to them. My heart sank on my second visit when I saw a family get turned away at a checkpoint after paying an exorbitant fee to a tout—they'd been completely scammed with useless fakes. Stick entirely to official municipal booking channels.

The security perimeters around the central historic core feel exactly like airport screening lanes. Security personnel will confiscate pocket lighters, matches, pocket knives, and large pressurized aerosol cans, so leave those back at your hotel room. Travel as light as possible to breeze through the security lines quickly, and keep an eye on your smartphone and backpack when caught in thick crowds.

The great booking game and how to play it

Thankfully, wandering through vibrant historic zones like the Nanluoguxiang hutongs, Yandai Xie Street, or the scenic willow-lined banks of Shichahai requires zero booking or entry fees. You can just walk right in. For the massive historic sites, though, the calendar rules everything. Here's exactly how the booking windows operate for the main spots:

Immersive cultural perspectives and imperial architecture preserved across the historic capital.

The Forbidden City (The Palace Museum): This is the toughest ticket to secure in town. Reservations open exactly 7 days in advance at 8:00 PM sharp through the official "故宫博物院" WeChat mini-program. My phone alarm is always set for 7:58 PM. I felt an incredible rush of adrenaline watching the reservation page freeze on my screen, followed by pure relief when the morning slot confirmation finally popped up. Morning entries cost 60 RMB during peak season and mean lighter crowds for photography. Remember, it's completely closed on Mondays.

Tiananmen Square and the Flag Raising Ceremony: Walking onto the square is completely free, but registration must be secured 1 to 7 days ahead via the "天安门广场预约参观" mini-program. If you want to see the famous sunrise flag-raising ceremony, select that specific early morning slot and get to the security gates at least an hour early, or you'll be staring at a wall of shoulders over a massive crowd.

Chairman Mao Memorial Hall: Admission is free, but you must reserve a spot. Tickets open 1 to 6 days ahead at 12:30 PM daily through its dedicated booking mini-program. It's only open in the morning, closed on Mondays, and expects total silence inside.

National Museum of China: Another fantastic free spot. Bookings open 1 to 7 days in advance at 5:00 PM daily on its official platform. It's closed on Mondays, and because the collection is absolutely massive, setting aside at least half a day to take it all in is a smart move.

Badaling Great Wall: This section gives you the longest planning window, opening slots up to 15 days ahead at 8:00 AM daily. You can use their official website or regular verified travel booking platforms to grab a timed entry pass. It gets incredibly packed during peak seasons, so I highly recommend biting the bullet, waking up early, and getting out there before the heat and the crowds peak.

The Summer Palace, Temple of Heaven, and Prince Gong's Mansion: These classic imperial parks are much easier to handle. Booking 3 to 5 days ahead on their official accounts is plenty. I like to go early in the morning to watch local residents practice Tai Chi and fly kites before the afternoon tour groups arrive.

My favorite bites across the neighborhoods

The aroma of roasting sesame, sizzling mutton fat, and sweet bean sauce hangs over certain districts like an irresistible cloud. Sitting down for authentic Peking Roast Duck is a total ritual. The duck arrives with incredibly crisp skin and tender meat, and the dining room usually buzzes with families and local groups sharing large tables. The chef carves the bird tableside, serving up impossibly crispy skin meant to be dipped lightly into white sugar, alongside tender breast meat that you wrap in thin wheat pancakes with sweet bean sauce, slivered scallions, and cool cucumber slices. Stay far away from the overpriced tourist traps sitting right outside major scenic gates. Instead, head to Siji Minfu for its reliably perfect crunch and great value, or Bianyifang to try their traditional closed-oven roasting style that keeps the meat incredibly juicy. Once they're done carving, ask them to fry up the remaining duck frame with salt-and-pepper spices instead of making soup—it's a massive flavor upgrade that blew me away.

Another soul-warming local favorite is Old Beijing Copper-Pot Mutton Shabu-Shabu. It uses a traditional charcoal-fired copper chimney pot filled with simple, clear boiling water. Because the broth is clean, it relies entirely on the quality of the fresh, hand-cut slices of mutton, which you swirl in the pot and dip into a rich, savory sesame paste mixed with sweet pickled garlic. For the real deal, check out Nanmen Shuanrou or the legendary Jubaoyuan in the heart of the Niujie neighborhood.

While trying traditional Miancha—a thick, comforting millet mush topped with savory sesame paste—at a crowded wooden bench, I naturally reached for a spoon. An elderly local gave me a warm, crinkly smile and gestured that the real old-school way is to hold the bowl and sip directly from the rim without utensils. I felt a little embarrassed but incredibly charmed by the interaction. Taking that first authentic bite of Zhajiangmian—chewy wheat noodles tossed with crisp greens and deep pork sauce—at Fangzhuanchang No. 69 or Haiwanju was pure joy. For the adventurous, Luzhu Huoshao (at Menkuang Hutong or 小肠陈) is a deeply savory, earthy stew of simmered pork intestines, lungs, and baked flatbread doused in garlic purée and hot chili oil. Do not skip BaoDu (quick-scalded tripe dipped in sesame sauce at 爆肚冯 or Jinshenglong), smooth Wandouhuang (sweet pea cake), or soft Lvdagun (glutinous rice rolls coated in roasted bean powder) at Huguosi Snacks or Jinfang Snacks.

Authentic culinary traditions ranging from imperial legacy roasts to classic local comfort dishes.

Stay far away from the commercialized Wangfujing Snack Street; it's an overpriced tourist hub with mediocre flavors. If you want real culinary history, take a walk down Niujie, the historic center for authentic Halal dining, where locals line up for fresh mutton cuts and sticky rice cakes. Stroll along Huguosi Street to find a high density of classic snack counters, or stop by Qianmen and Xianyukou for excellent sit-down options right near the center of town. If you want a younger vibe, the trendy stalls around Nanluoguxiang and Yandai Xiejie mix historic courtyard scenery with creative modern street snacks, making them perfect for grabbing a bite while taking photos. Just avoid the generic, overpriced food carts parked right by scenic entrances.

Riding the rails and choosing a home base

When it comes to getting around, the subway is your absolute best friend. It cruises right past the city's infamous gridlock and runs directly to almost every historic sight you'll want to see. Fares are based on distance, starting at an affordable 3 RMB and going up from there, while the specialized airport express lines are a flat 25 RMB. Just pull up your YiTongXing app and scan the QR code to pass through the turnstiles. The city buses are even cheaper, usually costing just 1 or 2 RMB per ride. But here's an important operational rule that caught me off guard on my first trip: you must scan your transit app both when you get on through the front door and right before you step off through the back door. I forgot to scan when exiting once and got charged the maximum route fare penalty on my account.

Keep the weekday rush hours in mind—the subways get incredibly packed from 07:30 to 09:00 AM and again from 5:00 to 7:00 PM. If you're not racing against a tight attraction booking deadline, give yourself a break and travel outside these windows. Also, the major transfer stations often have long, winding pedestrian tunnels between lines, so don't cut your travel times too close. If you're heading out to the Badaling Great Wall, ignore the unlicensed drivers trying to wave you into their cars on the street; hop on the official Suburban Railway S2 Line train or take an authorized tourist express bus from the central transit squares instead.

The number one rule for picking a hotel room in Beijing is simple: make sure it's within a short walk of a metro station entrance. The quiet inside a secluded courtyard alley just fifty meters off a roaring multi-lane avenue is magic. For first-timers, the Qianmen and Dashilan neighborhood is excellent; it sits right south of Tiananmen Square and has a deep mix of budget chains and unique boutique spots. Dongsi and Nanluoguxiang offer a dreamlike hutong atmosphere filled with independent cafes and stunning courtyard homestays. Xizhimen is a massive transit hub packed with practical domestic business hotels that offer great mid-range value, making it perfect if you want a straightforward, budget-friendly place to sleep. If shopping and nightlife are your priority, the gleaming towers of Guomao and Sanlitun host premium international luxury brands, though they sit further east from the historic core.

Economy chain brands like Hanting, Home Inn, or 7 Days usually cost between 100 and 300 RMB a night near metro stations, mid-range rooms run 300 to 600 RMB, and courtyard guesthouses swing wildly from 200 to well over 1,000 RMB. During peak travel seasons or major holidays, book far ahead to avoid surging prices or empty calendars. Returning to a warm, heated courtyard room after walking fifteen miles in a biting winter wind feels like absolute paradise.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do foreigners need to carry a physical passport in Beijing?
Yes, absolutely. Beijing runs on a strict real-name registration system. You must have your original physical passport on you at all times to clear security checks, buy attraction entry passes, and check into your hotel. Digital photos aren't accepted at the gates.
Can I use cash in Beijing, or is it entirely cashless?
The city runs almost entirely on mobile payments via apps like WeChat Pay and Alipay. You don't need to carry much paper currency; keeping just a few dozen RMB in small bills in your pocket for rare emergency backups is more than enough.
When should I book Forbidden City tickets?
Tickets open exactly 7 days in advance at 8:00 PM sharp through the official WeChat mini-program. They sell out incredibly fast, so you need to be logged into the app a few minutes before the release time to secure a slot.
Are major museums and historical sites open every day of the week?
No. The Forbidden City, National Museum of China, Chairman Mao Memorial Hall, and most public museums are strictly closed every Monday. Make sure to schedule your week around this day to avoid missing out.
How does the bus fare scanning system work?
Ordinary buses charge between 1 and 2 RMB, but you must scan your phone transit app twice: once when you enter through the front doors and once right before exiting through the back doors. Failing to scan out will result in your account being charged the maximum possible fare for that entire route.
Which neighborhood is best for a first-time visitor's hotel stay?
Qianmen and Dashilan are the top picks for first-timers. It puts you within walking distance or a short 1-2 stop metro ride of Tiananmen Square and the Forbidden City, with plenty of local restaurants and hotel choices ranging from basic economy chains to traditional boutique spots.
