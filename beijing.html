Beijing: What I Wish Someone Had Told Me Before I Went

Last updated: June 2026 · 10 min read

I almost didn't get into the Forbidden City on my first day. Not because it was sold out — though it nearly was — but because I genuinely had no idea you needed to book in advance. I found out at 10pm the night before, scrambled to get on the official WeChat mini program, and somehow found two remaining morning slots. That was the moment I realized Beijing requires actual preparation, not just showing up and figuring it out.

So here's what I know now, after doing it the hard way.

Getting Into the Forbidden City Without the Panic

The Meridian Gate — the main entrance to the Forbidden City. The queues here are nothing compared to what's inside.

Tickets open exactly 7 days in advance on the official Forbidden City mini program, every night at 8pm. Morning sessions fill up first — I'd say by 8:05pm for peak season. The morning slot is genuinely better anyway. Fewer people, softer light, and the crowds haven't hit critical mass yet. Afternoon sessions go faster because everyone's already inside from the morning.

Your passport is what you need to book and to get in. The name on the booking has to match exactly. I watched someone ahead of me get turned away at the gate because of a middle name discrepancy — genuinely painful to witness. Double-check before you leave your hotel. Tickets are ¥60 in peak season.

One thing I wasn't prepared for: the scale. The Forbidden City has 980 buildings and 8,700 rooms. I spent four hours there and still felt like I rushed. Enter from the south gate on Tiananmen Square. When you exit through the north gate, there's Jingshan Park right there — climb the hill for an aerial view of the golden rooftops. One of the better photos I took on the whole trip.

MONDAY WARNING

The Forbidden City, the National Museum, Mao's Mausoleum and most major museums are all closed on Mondays. I made this mistake once. Don't plan your big museum days on a Monday.

The Great Wall — Which Section and Why It Matters

Tiananmen Square — enormous, and busier than you expect even on weekdays.

Badaling is what everyone's heard of. It's also genuinely overwhelming on weekends — I'm talking shoulder-to-shoulder on the steps, people stopping to take photos every three seconds, the whole thing. I went anyway because I didn't know better the first time.

Mutianyu is the better call for most people. Well-restored, far less crowded, and there's a cable car if the steep sections look like too much. It takes about 90 minutes from central Beijing by bus or hired car. The wall itself opens at 7:30am — go early. Not "tourist early," actually early. The light before 9am is completely different from midday.

If you're into hiking and don't mind some planning, Jinshanling has crumbling unrestored sections that feel completely different from the tourist sections. You're essentially walking through ruins. More effort to get to, but the photos are unlike anything at Badaling.

One thing to watch for at all the Wall sections: anyone near the entrance offering "faster entry" or "skip the line" tickets. Every single one of them is either overcharging massively or selling you something that won't work. Book through the official site or Trip.com and ignore everyone who approaches you.

Tiananmen Square and the Mausoleum

Tiananmen at night — a completely different atmosphere from the daytime crowds.

The square itself is free but requires advance booking — up to 7 days ahead through the Tiananmen Square reservation mini program. If you want to see the flag-raising ceremony, book that separately in the same mini program and arrive at least an hour before. The crowds are significant. The ceremony itself is brief and oddly moving.

Mao's Mausoleum is free. Booking opens daily at 12:30pm for visits 1–6 days out. It's only open in the mornings — don't show up in the afternoon expecting to get in. Closed Mondays. Inside you're moving the whole time; no stopping, no photos. Keep quiet and follow the flow.

Food I Actually Recommend

Temple of Heaven — arrive before 9am to see locals doing tai chi in the park. By 10am it's tour groups.

Peking duck is the obvious one. I've had it at Quanjude and at Da Dong — Da Dong wins, not even close. The duck is leaner, less greasy, and the whole experience is better. It's on the expensive side but not absurd. Eat the skin with just white sugar first, then move to the pancake wraps with scallion, cucumber and hoisin. The carcass gets made into a soup at the end. Don't skip the soup.

For something more everyday: jianbing in the morning. It's a savory crepe — egg, crispy wonton, chili sauce, sometimes a sausage — made fresh in about two minutes by a street vendor. Costs maybe ¥10-15. I ate one almost every morning and never got tired of it. Look for the carts near subway entrances early in the morning.

Avoid the Wangfujing Snack Street. I know it looks interesting on maps. It's overpriced tourist food that bears no resemblance to what Beijing people actually eat. The hutong neighborhoods around Nanluoguxiang and Beihai Park have better options at honest prices.

If you want copper hotpot — the real Beijing version with clear broth and hand-cut lamb — try Nan Men Shuan Rou or Ju Bao Yuan on Niujie. The lamb is the point. The sesame sauce dip is the point. Order the sesame flatbread on the side.

Getting Around Without Losing Your Mind

Download the Yitong Xing app before you arrive — it handles metro and bus payments with a QR code and saves you dealing with a transit card. Alternatively, Alipay has a transit function that works the same way. Single metro journeys run ¥3-7 depending on distance. The airport express to the city center is ¥25.

Morning rush (7:30-9am) and evening rush (5-7pm) on weekdays are genuinely brutal at major interchange stations. If you can avoid them, do. The platforms at Xizhimen or Guomao during peak hours require a kind of spatial confidence I did not have on day one.

DiDi works fine and accepts foreign cards. Useful for late nights or when you're carrying luggage. Don't take taxis from people who approach you outside train stations.

Where to Stay

The front door area — Qianmen and Dashilar — puts you within walking distance of Tiananmen, the Forbidden City and Temple of Heaven. It's the obvious choice for a first visit and the most convenient for seeing the major sights. Hotels range from budget chains to mid-range, lots of options.

Dongsi and Nanluoguxiang if you want hutong atmosphere. Quieter, older feeling, small independent restaurants everywhere. Still good metro access. The guesthouses here are genuinely charming — narrow courtyards, brick walls, the sounds of the neighborhood at night.

Xizhimen is a major transport hub with multiple metro lines converging. Practical rather than atmospheric. Good if you're moving around a lot or arriving by high speed train. Chain hotels dominate.

One honest note on timing: book accommodation early if you're coming during Golden Week (first week of October) or May holidays. Prices go up significantly and the good places fill fast. Showing up without a booking during peak season is not a strategy I'd recommend.

The Practical Stuff That Actually Tripped Me Up

Carry your passport. Not a photo of it on your phone — the actual passport. Some sites accept a photo in a pinch but the Forbidden City and certain museums want the real thing. I left mine at the hotel once and had to go back. Annoying.

Beijing's weather is genuinely variable. Spring (April-May) and autumn (September-October) are the good seasons — mild, occasionally clear skies. Summer is hot and humid with the occasional heavy downpour. Winter can be very cold and the air quality drops. Whenever you go, comfortable shoes are not optional. I walked 20,000 steps on my first day without particularly trying.

The air quality varies a lot day to day. Download an AQI app and check before planning outdoor-heavy days. Some days are fine; other days you can taste it. On bad air days, indoor museums become more appealing.
